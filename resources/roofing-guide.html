What type of roof is standard on Connecticut homes?

The majority of Connecticut homes built between 1950 and 2000 are covered with asphalt shingles — either the older 3-tab style or the heavier architectural (dimensional) shingles that became dominant in the 1990s. Asphalt shingles remain the most common roofing material installed on CT homes today, accounting for over 85% of residential roof replacements in Fairfield County.

Older Connecticut homes — particularly colonial-era and Victorian-era houses in towns like Fairfield, Westport, Ridgefield, and Greenwich — may still have original slate roofs, which when properly maintained can last well over a century. Cedar shake roofs are common on upscale homes built from the 1970s through the 1990s, particularly in New Canaan, Darien, and Westport. Clay tile is rare in CT outside of Mediterranean-style properties.

New construction in Connecticut has trended strongly toward architectural shingles, with growing interest in Class 4 impact-resistant shingles (which qualify for insurance premium discounts from some carriers) and metal roofing for premium projects. Flat and low-slope roofs on additions, garages, and commercial properties are typically covered with EPDM rubber membrane or TPO.

How long do different roofing materials last in Connecticut's climate?

Connecticut's climate is among the most demanding in the country for roofing materials. A CT roof must withstand summer heat above 90°F, winter temperatures that drop below 0°F, significant snowfall, ice accumulation and freeze-thaw cycling, nor'easters with sustained winds over 50 mph, and coastal humidity along the Sound. Material lifespan estimates from manufacturers are based on average U.S. conditions; CT's extremes push some materials toward the lower end of their rated range.

What are the different parts of a CT home's roof system?

Understanding your roof as a system — not just as the visible shingles — is essential to making informed decisions about repair versus replacement, evaluating contractor proposals, and understanding what your insurance claim covers. Every component serves a specific function, and failure in any one component compromises the whole system.

Roof deck and sheathing

The structural foundation of the roofing system is the wood deck — typically oriented strand board (OSB) or plywood — nailed to the rafters. Every other component sits on top of the deck. Rotted, soft, or delaminated decking must be replaced before new roofing is installed; this is often discovered during tear-off and generates a legitimate supplement to the insurance estimate if storm-related. EER replaces decking by the sheet, pricing it out as a separate line item in every proposal.

Ice and water shield

Ice and water shield is a self-adhering rubberized asphalt membrane that bonds directly to the deck. In Connecticut, it is applied at the eaves (minimum 24 inches inside the wall line, typically 36–48 inches on low-pitch roofs), in all valleys, and around every penetration — chimneys, skylights, pipe boots, and wall intersections. It is the last line of defense when meltwater backs up behind an ice dam. CT building code mandates it; insurance carriers are required to cover it as a code upgrade on replacements where it was not present before.

Underlayment

Over the ice and water shield and across the remainder of the deck goes the underlayment — a secondary water-resistant layer. Traditional 15-lb or 30-lb felt paper has been largely replaced in quality installations by synthetic polypropylene underlayments that are lighter, stronger, more resistant to tearing in wind, and have much longer UV exposure windows during installation. EER specifies synthetic underlayment on all projects.

Drip edge and eave metal

Drip edge is an L-shaped aluminum or galvanized steel strip installed at the eave and rake edges of the roof. It directs water off the deck edge into the gutter rather than allowing it to wick back under the shingle. CT building code requires drip edge on new installations and full replacements. On older roofs, drip edge was not always installed — its absence is a code upgrade item that insurance carriers must cover when a storm-damaged roof is being replaced.

Field shingles

The visible surface of the roof. For asphalt shingles, EER's standard specification is GAF Timberline HDZ — a laminated architectural shingle with a factory-sealed LayerLock tab system that provides rated wind resistance to 130 mph. Class 4 impact-resistant options are available at a premium and recommended for areas with documented hail history.

Ridge cap

The apex of every roof slope is covered with specially cut or purpose-made ridge cap shingles. Ridge cap is a separate line item in both contractor proposals and insurance estimates. On a full replacement, the ridge cap is always replaced. GAF ridge cap shingles match the Timberline system and carry the same warranty.

Flashing system

Flashing is the most detail-intensive part of any roofing installation and the most common point of failure on aging roofs. Step flashing is installed course-by-course at wall intersections — each piece of L-shaped metal sits behind a shingle and laps over the one below. Counter-flashing is embedded into mortar joints or sealed into siding above the step flashing line. Valley flashing protects the horizontal channel where two roof planes meet — open metal, woven, or closed cut valleys each have different performance characteristics in CT's ice and snow conditions. Pipe boot flashings seal around plumbing vent penetrations. Skylight flashings are some of the most complex details on any CT roof. Every one of these components is inspected and, where necessary, replaced on an EER project.

Ventilation system

Proper attic ventilation is critical to shingle longevity and to preventing ice dams. The system works on convection: cooler outside air enters through soffit vents at the eave, flows along the underside of the deck, and exits through ridge vents or box vents at the peak. A properly ventilated attic in CT should remain close to outside temperature in winter — eliminating the heat differential that causes snow to melt on the upper roof and refreeze at the eave. One of the most common errors on CT reroof projects is installing a ridge vent without adequately sizing the soffit intake — which actually reduces ventilation effectiveness by creating a closed-circuit path.

Gutters and downspouts

Gutters are technically separate from the roofing system but are functionally inseparable from it. Clogged or inadequate gutters cause water to back up against the fascia, ice to dam at the eave, and water to infiltrate behind the gutter into the soffit and fascia board. On every roofing project, EER inspects and addresses gutters. George's Seamless Gutters — EER's parent company — is a seamless gutter specialist, so homeowners can address both roofing and gutters in the same project under a single contractor relationship.

How much does a new roof cost in Connecticut in 2025?

CT roofing costs are meaningfully higher than national averages, reflecting the combination of high labor costs, strict permitting requirements, and the full-system installation standards that CT code and climate demand. Homeowners who receive quotes significantly below these ranges should scrutinize the scope carefully — they are likely not getting synthetic underlayment, proper ice and water shield coverage, and drip edge, or the crew is not covered by adequate insurance.

Factors that adjust CT roof replacement cost

Pitch multiplier: Steeper roofs require more labor time, safety equipment, and material staging. A 12/12 pitch carries roughly a 35–40% labor premium over a 5/12. Tear-off layers: CT building code limits roof layers to two. A second tear-off of an existing layer adds material disposal cost and labor. Decking replacement: Rotted or damaged sheathing is replaced by the sheet; most projects replace 5–15% of the deck. Chimney complexity: Step and counter-flashing around a full masonry chimney is a half-day labor item. Multiple chimneys, decorative dormers, or multiple roof planes all increase labor time. Permit fees: CT municipality permit fees range from $75 to over $400 depending on the town and assessed project value. EER includes permit fees in every estimate and handles the filing.

What should I look for when hiring a roofing contractor in Connecticut?

Connecticut roofing has a persistent problem with post-storm out-of-state contractors — colloquially called "storm chasers" — who arrive after significant weather events offering fast repairs at attractive prices. They generate more complaints at the CT DCP than almost any other home improvement category. Here is exactly what to verify before signing any roofing contract in CT.

Verify the CT HIC license

Any CT home improvement contractor working on projects over $200 is required to hold a CT Home Improvement Contractor license issued by the Department of Consumer Protection. The license is searchable at ct.gov/dcp. Search by contractor name and verify the license number matches what appears on the contract. Unlicensed contractors have no bond, no complaint mechanism, and no oversight. Energy Efficient Roofer's license is CT HIC.0672649 — you can verify it right now.

Request a Certificate of Insurance (COI)

Ask for a current Certificate of Insurance showing two separate coverages: general liability insurance (minimum $1 million per occurrence) and workers' compensation. Both must name your address as the certificate holder. An unlicensed or underinsured contractor working on your roof creates personal liability exposure for you as the property owner if a worker is injured. Do not accept verbal assurances — insist on the actual certificate before work begins.

Get written, itemized proposals

A legitimate contractor proposal will specify the brand and model of every material being installed, the areas receiving ice and water shield, the type of underlayment, whether drip edge is included, how many layers are being torn off, and how debris disposal is handled. Vague proposals that describe "replace roof" without material specifications are a significant red flag. When comparing multiple quotes, verify that each is actually specifying the same scope — a $2,000 price difference often traces to one contractor omitting ice and water shield or the permit fee that another includes.

Understand the warranty structure

There are three distinct warranties on a roofing project: the manufacturer's materials warranty (covers defective shingles), the contractor's workmanship warranty (covers installation errors), and the manufacturer's extended system warranty (like GAF's System Plus, which requires a certified installer and covers both materials and related accessories as an integrated system). EER's workmanship warranty and GAF Certified Plus status qualify homeowners for the enhanced GAF System Plus warranty. Standard contractor workmanship warranties in CT typically run two to five years; EER's terms are specified in every contract.

They offer to file your insurance claim, waive your deductible (illegal in CT), and rush your project before the season changes. How do I know when my CT roof needs replacement vs. repair?

This is the most common question Mike answers at free inspections — and it is one where a contractor's honest answer matters a great deal, because the difference between a $1,200 repair and a $14,000 replacement is significant. EER's approach is to give the homeowner a clear, documented picture of the roof's current condition and its realistic remaining service life, without pushing replacement when repair genuinely makes sense.

Signs that suggest repair

Localized damage from a single incident — a few shingles blown off in a specific area, a pipe boot that has cracked, a valley that has lifted — on an otherwise sound roof is a repair situation. If the field shingles across the majority of the roof are still sealed, granule coverage is adequate, and the sheathing shows no signs of compromise, targeted repair can add meaningful years to a roof that is performing well in the undamaged areas.

Signs that indicate replacement

Several conditions indicate a roof is at or past its functional service life and should be replaced rather than repeatedly repaired: widespread granule loss exposing the dark asphalt mat on multiple slopes; curling or cupping across the field (not localized to a damaged area); visible daylight in the attic through the deck; sheathing soft spots when walked; multiple previous repairs on a roof over 20 years old; and active moss or algae that has penetrated through the shingle layer to the deck. When a roof is within five years of the end of its rated life and has suffered storm damage, replacement is almost always the better economic decision — particularly if it is insurable.

The age-condition matrix

EER uses a simple framework at inspections: if the roof is under 15 years old and the damage is isolated, repair. If the roof is 15–20 years old with isolated damage, repair if the overall condition grades well and the remaining shingles are still sealed. If the roof is 20+ years old, regardless of whether the immediate damage is isolated, a full inspection of the entire deck condition is warranted before committing to repair — you may be putting $1,500 in repair costs into a roof that will need full replacement within two to three years.

What CT building code requirements apply to roof replacement?

Connecticut has adopted the International Residential Code (IRC) with state-specific amendments. The relevant provisions for residential roof replacement in CT include the following requirements that affect both the scope and cost of every reroof project.

Ice and water shield requirements

The IRC as adopted in Connecticut requires self-adhering ice and water shield from the eave upslope to a point at least 24 inches inside the interior warm wall line of the building. In practice, on a standard CT overhang, this means roughly two to three courses of ice and water shield at the eave. Most quality contractors — and EER specifically — extend coverage to 36–48 inches on pitches below 6/12 and apply it in all valleys regardless of pitch. Ice and water shield is also required around all penetrations and at all valleys under the IRC.

Drip edge requirements

The 2015 IRC (and subsequent adopted editions) requires drip edge at both eaves and rakes. Many older CT homes have drip edge at the eave only or no drip edge at all. When a full replacement is performed, drip edge is required at all edges — this is a code upgrade item that insurance carriers are required to include in the claim scope when the original roof did not have it.

Permit requirements

Most CT municipalities require a building permit for a full roof replacement. Some towns accept a notified repair for minor work; others require permits for any shingle replacement over a certain percentage of the roof area. EER handles permit filing for all projects and coordinates the final inspection where required. The permit fee is a legitimate project cost included in every EER estimate and in every insurance supplement where it is not already on the carrier's estimate.

Layer limits

CT building code (IRC R905.1.1) permits a maximum of two layers of asphalt shingles on a residential roof. If your roof currently has two layers, the full existing covering must be torn off before new shingles are installed. This is both a code requirement and a quality standard — installing over two existing layers means the new shingles cannot bond flat, ventilation is compromised, and the total weight load on the deck is increased beyond design parameters.

How do I maintain my Connecticut roof to maximize lifespan?

A well-maintained CT roof will reach the upper end of its rated lifespan; a neglected roof in the same climate will fail years early. Roof maintenance is not complex, but it requires consistency — twice-yearly inspections and addressing small issues before they compound into major ones.

Annual inspection — spring and fall

Walk the perimeter of your home twice a year and look at the roof from the ground with binoculars. In spring after the first severe winter, check for lifted ridge cap, missing shingles, exposed flashing at chimneys or sidewalls, and gutter damage from ice loads. In fall before winter, verify that gutters are clear, no moss or algae growth is visible, and no overhanging branches are in contact with the roof surface. EER offers free annual roof inspections to existing customers.

Gutter cleaning

Clean gutters at least twice per year — once in late fall after leaf drop and once in early spring. Clogged gutters in CT winter become an ice accumulation point that forces water back against the fascia and under the eave flashing. This is a primary driver of ice dam formation. In areas with heavy tree coverage, quarterly cleaning may be necessary.

Branch trimming

Overhanging branches that contact or nearly contact the roof surface cause two problems: the physical abrasion of branches on shingles accelerates granule loss, and branches deposit leaf debris and moisture that accelerates moss and algae growth. Trim branches to maintain a minimum three-foot clearance from the roof surface. Large branches over the roof also create a falling object risk during ice storms — a category of damage covered by insurance but better prevented entirely.

Flashing and sealant

Flashing failures account for a disproportionate share of CT roof leaks. The most common failure points are pipe boot flashings (the rubber collar around plumbing vents cracks after 10–15 years in CT temperature cycles), chimney counter-flashing (mortar joint failures are gradual and invisible until water is inside the wall), and step flashing at sidewalls where paint or siding covers the lap. Address any known flashing anomaly immediately — water intrusion is exponentially easier and cheaper to stop before it has been running for one to two years.

Attic ventilation check

Once per year — ideally in early winter — go into the attic on a cold day. You should not see frost on the underside of the decking. Frost in the attic means warm, moist interior air is reaching the cold deck and condensing — a sign of inadequate attic ventilation or air sealing. Over time, persistent attic condensation causes decking rot and premature shingle failure from above. If you see frost, the ventilation system needs to be evaluated before the problem escalates.

Frequently asked questions — Connecticut roofing

What is the CT Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) license?

The CT HIC license is issued by the Department of Consumer Protection and is required for any home improvement project over $200. The license number must appear on every contract. Always verify a contractor's license at ct.gov/dcp before signing anything. Energy Efficient Roofer's license is CT HIC.0672649.

What is an ice and water shield and is it required in Connecticut?

Ice and water shield is a self-adhering waterproof membrane installed under shingles at vulnerable areas — eaves, valleys, and around all roof penetrations. Connecticut building code requires it in most jurisdictions for full roof replacements. It provides the last layer of protection against ice dam water backup, which is a common source of interior water damage in CT winters. Any proposal that does not include ice and water shield is either a substandard spec or will fail CT permit inspection.

What is the best roofing brand for Connecticut homes?

GAF is the largest roofing manufacturer in North America and produces the most widely installed shingles in Connecticut. CertainTeed and Owens Corning are also quality products. Material brand is less important than installation quality — even a premium shingle will fail early with poor attic ventilation, improper ice and water shield coverage, or sloppy flashing. What is the GAF System Plus warranty?

GAF's System Plus warranty covers qualifying shingles and accessories for 50 years when installed by a GAF certified contractor using a complete GAF system. EER's GAF Certified Plus status qualifies homeowners for this warranty on applicable product lines. The System Plus warranty covers both manufacturing defects in the shingles and workmanship-related failures — a broader protection than a standard manufacturer's warranty, which covers materials only.

How do I file a complaint against a roofing contractor in Connecticut?

Contact the CT Department of Consumer Protection at ct.gov/dcp. Complaints can be filed online. Licensed contractors are subject to DCP investigation, and sustained complaints can result in license suspension or revocation. For insurance claim disputes involving a carrier, contact the CT Insurance Department. Always get every aspect of your project in writing — contract, scope, material specs, and warranty terms — before work begins.

How do I find NOAA storm data to support a CT insurance claim?

Go to the NOAA Storm Events Database at ncei.noaa.gov/access/storm-events/ and search by state, county, and date range. This provides official federal documentation of wind speed, hail diameter, and storm track. It is the primary reference used in insurance supplement packages to establish that a storm event occurred, that hail of a specific size was recorded in your county, and that the damage on your roof is consistent with the documented event.
