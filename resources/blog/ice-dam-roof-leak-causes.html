Why New Roofs Still Leak from Ice Dams — The Real Causes After 200 CT Jobs

This past season I worked approximately 200 Connecticut homes with ice dam damage — all documented with before-and-after photo records in our CompanyCam system. What I found challenges the assumption most homeowners — and many contractors — make: that roof condition is the primary driver of whether a roof leaks from an ice dam. It isn't. Some of the worst leaks I repaired were on roofs installed within the last 12 months. Here's what actually causes ice dam leaks, and why it doesn't matter whether your roof is 2 years old or 20.

What Is an Ice Dam and Why Does It Cause Leaks?

An ice dam is a ridge of ice that forms at the eave of a roof after a snowfall followed by a freeze-thaw cycle. The mechanism: heat escaping from the living space through the ceiling and into the attic warms the roof deck. That warm deck melts the snow sitting above it. The meltwater runs down the slope toward the eave — which overhangs the exterior wall and sits above outdoor air rather than the warm attic — and refreezes. The ice builds up. More snowmelt runs down and can't drain past the ice. It backs up, and water finds its way under the shingles, through the deck, and into the home.

The critical point: the ice dam itself is a symptom. The underlying disease is heat loss from the house into the attic. Stop the heat loss, stop the ice dam. Everything else — the shingles, the roof age, the gutter condition — is secondary.

The Finding: Roof Condition Was Not the Primary Driver

After working roughly 200 ice dam jobs in a single CT season, a clear pattern emerged. I went into homes where the roof was 18 years old and showing significant wear — cracked shingles, granule loss, aging flashing — and the roof had not leaked at all. I went into homes where the roof was 11 months old, installed by another contractor the previous fall, and the homeowner had water running down their interior walls.

The age and condition of the shingles had almost nothing to do with whether the roof leaked. What mattered was the three factors below — in that order.

Cause 1: Poor Attic Ventilation

Ventilation controls whether the roof deck stays close to outdoor air temperature or whether it warms up and melts snow. A properly ventilated attic — continuous airflow from soffit to ridge at the correct Net Free Area ratio — keeps the deck cold. Cold deck, no snowmelt, no ice dam.

An under-ventilated attic traps heat. The deck warms. Snow melts. The cycle starts. I've walked into homes with brand-new roofing and found gable vents as the only exhaust, puck-style soffit vents providing a fraction of the required intake area, or soffit vents that had been painted shut over years of exterior repaints. In every case, the ventilation system was essentially nonfunctional — and the new shingles made no difference because the heat source driving the ice dam was still present and unaddressed.

This is the contractor's responsibility during a reroofing job. Before you put new shingles on a house, you assess the ventilation system. If it's inadequate — and on CT homes built before 2000, it usually is — you fix it as part of the scope. A contractor who doesn't do this is selling you a new roof that will repeat the same failure cycle.

Cause 2: Poor Insulation at the Ceiling Plane

Ventilation addresses airflow through the attic. Insulation addresses how much heat gets into the attic in the first place. These are two separate systems and both matter.

Connecticut's recommended attic insulation level is R-49 to R-60. Most homes built before 1990 fall significantly short of this. Heat from the living space conducts through under-insulated ceiling planes into the attic space. Better ventilation helps exhaust that heat, but if the heat input is large enough, ventilation alone can't compensate. Addressing insulation at the air sealing level — particularly around recessed lights, attic hatches, plumbing and electrical penetrations through the top plate — is often more effective per dollar than adding ventilation square footage.

On several of the worst ice dam jobs this season, the attic had been recently reinsulated with blown cellulose — but the air sealing step had been skipped. The insulation had good R-value but air was bypassing it through unsealed penetrations. Heat was getting into the attic in quantity, the ventilation couldn't keep up, and the deck was running warm all winter.

Cause 3: Minimum-Standard Flashing — Especially at Walls

This is the one that produces the most dramatic failures on newer roofs. Ice and water shield is required under Connecticut building code at roof eaves — the bottom 24 inches, measured from inside the exterior wall. Most contractors meet this requirement and stop there. That is the minimum standard. On a house with complex geometry — dormers, additions, roof planes that terminate at a vertical sidewall — the minimum standard is not enough.

Here's where the new roofs failed: wherever a roof plane meets a vertical wall surface — a dormer cheek, a sidewall above a lower roof, the wall above a garage addition — ice and water shield needs to run up that wall. Not just across the bottom of the roof. Up the wall. In the CT climate, ice backs up along wall intersections just as aggressively as it backs up at eaves. If the ice and water shield stops at the roof plane rather than extending up the adjacent wall behind the step flashing, backed-up water finds the gap and enters.

I saw this on multiple roofs installed within the previous 12 months. The eave detail was correct. The field installation looked fine. But at every sidewall intersection, the membrane stopped at the roof plane. The winter gave us the conditions to back water up to exactly that termination point, and it came right in. The shingles were perfect. The code minimums were technically met. The roof leaked.

What This Means for Roof Age and Condition

Roof condition was not irrelevant — it was just secondary. A roof that's already compromised by age, cracked shingles, or prior damage has a higher probability of leaking during an ice dam event. The underlying conditions — ventilation, insulation, flashing — set the base probability. Roof condition acts as a multiplier.

This means a homeowner with a 20-year-old roof on a well-ventilated, well-insulated house with correct wall flashing may have lower ice dam leak risk than the neighbor with a 2-year-old roof installed over a poorly ventilated attic with minimum flashing. Counterintuitive, but that's what the job data showed.

It also means that if you're replacing a roof that has leaked from ice dams, replacing the shingles without addressing the ventilation and insulation will produce the same result on the new roof. The sales cycle just resets to the next contractor who installs new shingles on the same broken system.

This Applies from Starter Homes to Multi-Million Dollar Builds

One more finding worth stating plainly: the ice dam failures this season ran the full spectrum of CT home values — from entry-level capes to multi-million dollar custom builds in Greenwich and Westport. Home value does not protect you from ice dam damage.

In fact, expensive custom homes often have more complex roof geometry than simpler colonial stock plans — more dormers, more sidewall intersections, more opportunities for minimum flashing standards to be insufficient. A complex roofline on a $3 million home with an under-ventilated attic and ice and water shield that stops at the eave rather than running up the adjacent walls will fail just as predictably as the starter cape three towns over.

The builders and roofers who work on high-value homes are not immune to meeting minimum standards. The homeowner paying premium prices has every right to expect and demand above-minimum performance — and should specifically ask whether ice and water shield will be run up wall intersections, not just along the eave.

What to Ask Your Roofer Before Signing

Whether you're dealing with active ice dam damage or planning a reroofing project on a home with a history of winter leaks, ask these specific questions:

  1. Will you assess and correct my attic ventilation as part of this project? If the answer is "the ventilation looks fine" without an NFA calculation, push back.
  2. Where exactly will the ice and water shield be installed? The correct answer includes: all eaves (minimum 24 inches inside the exterior wall), all valleys, around all penetrations, and up all vertical wall intersections where a roof plane meets a sidewall or dormer.
  3. What is my attic's current R-value and air sealing condition? A roofer can't answer the insulation question, but it tells you whether you need a separate insulation contractor involved before or after the roof.

At Energy Efficient Roofer, the ventilation assessment is part of every inspection. We don't install new shingles on a broken ventilation system and call it done. The ice dam jobs this past season reinforced exactly why that standard exists.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a brand new roof leak from an ice dam?

Yes. After working approximately 200 Connecticut ice dam jobs in a single season, some of the worst leaks were on roofs installed within the last 12 months. The shingles themselves weren't the problem. The failures came from poor attic ventilation, inadequate insulation, and flashing installed to minimum standards — specifically, ice and water shield not being run up wall surfaces where a roof plane meets a vertical wall. A new roof installed without addressing ventilation and insulation will leak from ice dams just as reliably as an old one.

What causes ice dam leaks on Connecticut homes?

Based on inspecting approximately 200 CT homes with ice dam damage in a single season, the primary causes in order: (1) Poor attic ventilation — heat escapes from the living space, warms the roof deck, melts snow, and the meltwater refreezes at the cold eave. (2) Poor insulation — insufficient R-value and air sealing at the ceiling plane allows heat into the attic. (3) Minimum-standard flashing — ice and water shield installed only at the eave but not run up vertical walls at dormers, additions, and sidewall intersections. Roof age and condition were secondary — they raised the probability but were not the root cause.

Does an ice dam mean my roof needs to be replaced?

Not necessarily. If the roof is otherwise in good condition, addressing the root causes — ventilation, insulation, and flashing — may resolve recurring ice dam leaks without a full replacement. If the roof is already compromised by age or prior damage, combining replacement with ventilation and insulation upgrades is the right approach. The key question is whether roof condition is a contributing factor or incidental.

Why did my new roof leak during winter even though it was just installed?

A new roof that leaks from ice dams almost always has one or more of these issues: attic ventilation was not assessed or corrected during installation; ice and water shield was not run up vertical wall surfaces at dormers or sidewall intersections; or the installation met minimum code but not the standard required to resist CT ice conditions. Installing new shingles over an under-ventilated attic in a poorly insulated house produces a new roof that will leak from ice dams just as reliably as the one it replaced.

Does ice dam damage apply to expensive homes in Connecticut?

Yes. In a single season working approximately 200 ice dam jobs, failures ran from starter homes to multi-million dollar custom builds. Expensive homes often have more complex roof geometry — dormers, multiple roof planes meeting walls — creating more opportunities for minimum flashing to fail. A $3 million custom home with inadequate ventilation and ice and water shield that stops at the eave will leak just as reliably as a $250,000 cape.
