How to Read a Roofing Estimate in Connecticut — Line by Line

Most Connecticut homeowners accept a roofing estimate without understanding what they're actually buying. They compare the bottom-line number across three bids and choose the middle one. The problem: two estimates with the same total price can represent completely different scopes of work — one of them will last 30 years, the other will leak in five. Here's how to read an estimate line by line, what must be there, and what's missing in a bad one.

What Every Legitimate CT Roofing Estimate Must Include

A properly itemized roofing estimate is a complete scope of work. Every component of the roofing system should appear as a separate line item. If you receive a one-page quote that says "roof replacement — 28 squares — $9,500," you have no way of knowing what you're getting. Here's what should be listed:

Square Footage Calculation

The estimate should state the number of squares (1 square = 100 sq ft of roof surface) and explain how that number was calculated. A reputable contractor measures the roof from the ground using satellite measurement tools or walks it with a measuring wheel and accounts for pitch factor — a 6/12 pitched roof has more surface area than a flat-mapped footprint suggests. If the square count seems suspiciously low or high compared to your home's footprint, ask to see the measurement breakdown.

Tear-Off and Disposal

Full tear-off of existing shingles (and layers) should be a separate line item, as should dumpster or disposal fees. This is not included in the shingle cost. In Connecticut, most municipalities have specific requirements around shingle disposal, and the contractor is responsible for proper handling. If disposal is missing from the estimate, ask whether it's bundled somewhere — or whether the crew is planning to haul it to a neighbor's dumpster.

Underlayment Specification

The estimate should name the underlayment product. Felt 15 lb, felt 30 lb, and synthetic underlayment are not the same thing. In Connecticut's climate, synthetic underlayment is the preferred standard — it's more tear-resistant during installation, has better water resistance, and lies flatter for a cleaner finished appearance. An estimate that says "underlayment" without specifying the type is a gap you should fill before signing.

Ice and Water Shield Specification

Connecticut code requires ice and water shield at eaves, valleys, and around penetrations. The estimate should state: where it will be applied, the product name, and how many squares/linear feet are included. Skipping ice and water shield is the most common way cheap contractors shave money off a bid — it's also the most likely place your roof will fail the first winter. Do not accept an estimate without this line item spelled out.

Drip Edge — Material and Color

Drip edge is the metal strip along the eaves and rakes that directs water off the roof and away from the fascia. It must be listed with the material (aluminum or galvanized steel) and the color. Missing drip edge is a code violation in most CT municipalities and accelerates fascia rot. The color should match or complement your soffit and fascia for a finished appearance.

Valley Material

Open metal valleys (W-metal or Z-bar) and closed-cut shingle valleys are both acceptable in CT. The estimate should specify which method will be used and with what material. Open metal valleys are more durable and easier to service. The line item should name the metal gauge and type.

Field Shingles — Manufacturer, Product Name, Color, Wind Rating

This is the most important specification line. The estimate must state: manufacturer (GAF, CertainTeed, Owens Corning, etc.), product name (not just "architectural shingle"), color name, wind rating, and weight class. Two contractors might both say "architectural shingles" — one might mean a GAF Timberline HDZ with a 130 mph wind rating and a Lifetime limited warranty, the other might mean a builder-grade entry product with a 60 mph wind rating and a 25-year warranty. Same category, completely different product. Always confirm the exact product name before signing.

Ridge Cap Shingles

Ridge cap is a separate product from field shingles. It should be listed as its own line item with a product name — ideally a manufacturer-specific hip and ridge cap designed to match your field shingle. Some contractors use cut-down field shingles as ridge cap, which is a visible shortcut that also reduces weather performance at the most exposed part of the roof.

Pipe Boots and Penetration Flashing

Every plumbing vent stack, exhaust vent, and penetration through the roof needs its own flashing or boot. The estimate should list the number of penetrations, the type of flashing (lead, neoprene/rubber, metal), and whether existing flashing is being replaced or re-used. Reusing 20-year-old pipe boots on a new roof is asking for a leak in Year 2 — ask specifically whether all penetration flashing is being replaced.

Starter Strip

Starter strip is a separate line item — it's the adhesive-backed piece installed at the eaves and rakes before the first course of shingles. It creates the seal that prevents wind uplift at the most vulnerable edge of the roof. It's inexpensive, but its absence is a code violation and a wind damage waiting to happen in CT's nor'easter season.

Ventilation

Ridge vents, box vents, or power ventilators should be listed separately. If the existing ventilation is being maintained, the estimate should confirm what's there and that it meets the 1/150 or 1/300 net-free-area requirement (more on that in our energy efficiency article). If ventilation is being added or upgraded, the type and quantity should be specified.

Permit

The permit should appear as a line item. In CT, a full roof replacement requires a building permit. Period. If the permit isn't on the estimate, ask about it explicitly. If the contractor tells you a permit isn't needed, verify that with your local building department before work begins.

Red Flags in a Roofing Estimate

Missing Line Items

The most dangerous estimates are the ones with missing lines — not wrong numbers. If ice and water shield, drip edge, or permits aren't on the estimate, assume they're not being done. Asking "is it included?" after the fact won't protect you legally if the contract doesn't specify it.

Vague "Labor" Lump Sum

An estimate that says "Labor — $3,500" with no further breakdown tells you nothing about what's being done. Labor should be broken out by phase (tear-off, installation, flashing work, cleanup) or bundled with the materials they install (e.g., "Tear-off and disposal: $X" and "Shingle installation: $X/sq"). A lump-sum labor line is a way to obscure what's actually being done.

No Manufacturer Product Specification

If the estimate says "30-year architectural shingles" without naming the manufacturer and product, you have no legal recourse if a sub-grade product is installed. Always get the product name in writing before you sign. The contract should match the estimate — if the language is vague in one place, tighten it before execution.

Price Per Square vs. Total Project Price — How to Compare Bids Fairly

When you have three bids with different total prices, the only fair comparison is price per square with equivalent scope. Divide each bid total by the number of squares to get a per-square cost. Then confirm: do all three bids include the same product grade, the same underlayment, ice and water shield, a permit? If one bid is $200/square cheaper, find the line item where they cut that cost. It will be there.

Unusually low bids — below $450/square in the current CT market — almost always mean a missing component, a subcontractor performing the work without oversight, or a contractor who will request additional payment after tear-off to address "discovered" deck issues that a proper inspection would have found before the bid was written.

The Warranty Line — What's Really Being Promised

Every roofing estimate has a warranty section. Read it carefully. There are two completely different warranties at play: the manufacturer's materials warranty and the contractor's workmanship warranty.

The manufacturer's materials warranty covers defects in the shingles themselves — premature granule loss, delamination, manufacturing defects. It does not cover installation errors. That warranty runs from the manufacturer and is transferred to the homeowner at registration. GAF's standard warranty is a 30-year limited warranty; their System Plus warranty extends to a 50-year limited warranty when installed by a certified contractor using a full GAF system.

The workmanship warranty is what the contractor provides on their installation. If the roof leaks because of how it was installed — improper flashing, missed nail pattern, inadequate ice and water shield application — the workmanship warranty is what makes the contractor return and fix it. Industry standard in CT is 2 to 5 years for workmanship. GAF Certified Plus contractors can offer up to 10 years of contractor labor coverage. Ask specifically how long the workmanship warranty is, get it in writing, and confirm the contractor will still be in business to honor it.

How an Insurance Estimate Differs from a Contractor Estimate

If you're filing a storm damage claim, you'll receive an estimate from your insurance carrier's adjuster (often generated in Xactimate software) in addition to contractor bids. These two documents are rarely identical — and the gap between them is not necessarily wrong.

The insurance estimate represents what the carrier initially agrees to pay. It often uses depreciated values and may miss line items (code upgrades like drip edge and ice and water shield, for example). A contractor estimate based on current material costs and full code compliance will frequently be higher. The difference is recoverable through a supplement — an additional claim to the insurance company for scope or price items not in the original estimate. A contractor experienced in insurance work (like Energy Efficient Roofer) will review the adjuster estimate against their own scope and identify every missing or undervalued line item for the supplement process.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a fair price per square for a roof replacement in Connecticut?

In Connecticut, a fair price for architectural shingle roof replacement typically ranges from $550 to $850 per square (100 sq ft) for a standard residential roof, depending on complexity, pitch, material grade, and local market conditions. Unusually low bids — under $450 per square — usually mean missing components (no ice and water shield, no permit, substandard underlayment) or a contractor who will disappear before the job is finished. Get three itemized bids and compare scope, not just price.

What is the difference between a materials warranty and a workmanship warranty on a roof?

A manufacturer's materials warranty covers defects in the shingle itself — delamination, premature granule loss, manufacturing defects. It does not cover installation errors. A workmanship warranty is provided by the contractor and covers their installation — if the roof leaks because of how it was installed, the workmanship warranty is what makes them come back. Always ask for both, in writing, before you sign. GAF Certified Plus contractors like Energy Efficient Roofer can offer the System Plus warranty, which covers both materials and installation through GAF.

Does a CT roofing contractor need to pull a permit?

Yes. In Connecticut, full roof replacements generally require a building permit from the local building department. The permit requires an inspection that confirms the work meets CT building code — including ice and water shield installation, drip edge, ventilation requirements, and deck condition. A contractor who tells you a permit isn't needed for a full tear-off and replacement is either wrong or trying to avoid the inspection. Always confirm the permit is pulled under your address before work begins.
