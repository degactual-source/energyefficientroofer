How to Read a Roofing Estimate (And Spot the Red Flags)

Roofing February 20, 2026 5 min read

You've gotten three roofing estimates and they're all different. One is much cheaper, one is much higher, and one is in the middle. Which one is right? The answer isn't always obvious—a low price can mean a bad deal, and a high price doesn't guarantee quality. Understanding what should be in a roofing estimate and what red flags to watch for will help you make the right choice.

What a Professional Estimate Should Include

A detailed, professional roofing estimate should be specific, transparent, and broken down into clear line items. Here's what to look for:

Materials Line Items

  • Shingles: Type, color, brand, and quantity (usually in "squares"—100 sq ft units). The estimate should specify the exact product (e.g., "Architectural shingles, Timberline HD, charcoal, 25 squares")
  • Underlayment: Type and quantity. This is the protective layer under the shingles. Specify whether it's synthetic, felt, or another material
  • Ice shield: Essential in Connecticut. Should be specified by linear feet and brand
  • Flashing: New metal flashing where the roof meets walls, chimneys, skylights, and valleys. Should be itemized by location and material
  • Ridge vent or ventilation: Proper roof ventilation is critical. Should be itemized separately
  • Nails: Roofing nails, typically specified as boxes or by count
  • Other hardware: Drip edge, starter shingles, or other necessary materials

Labor Line Items

  • Removal and disposal: Cost to remove old roofing material and haul it away. Should be itemized separately
  • Installation: Cost to install new roofing, typically quoted per square (100 sq ft)
  • Structural repairs: If the estimate includes replacing decking, repairs to trusses, or other structural work, these should be itemized with specifics

Additional Costs

  • Permits: Building permits required by your town (costs vary by jurisdiction but should be included)
  • Inspection fees: If your town requires a final inspection
  • Scaffolding or equipment: If necessary for safety or access
  • Roof protection: Temporary tarping or coverings during work

Important Details

  • Square footage or total squares: The estimate should clearly state the total roof area being replaced
  • Timeline: When the work will be completed
  • Warranty information: Both the manufacturer's warranty on materials and the contractor's workmanship warranty
  • Insurance and licensing: Contractor name, license number, and proof of insurance
  • Payment terms: When payment is due (deposit, progress payments, final payment)

Red Flags: What to Watch For

No Line Items or Vague Descriptions

An estimate that says "Roof replacement - $10,000" with no breakdown is not a real estimate. You have no way to verify costs or understand what's included. A professional contractor will break down every component. If a contractor won't provide details, ask. If they refuse, move on.

The Price Is Too Low

If one estimate is significantly cheaper than others (say, 30-40% below market), be suspicious. Roofing materials and labor don't vary dramatically. A too-low price usually means:

  • The contractor will cut corners (cheap materials, inadequate underlayment, poor ventilation)
  • They're inexperienced or desperate for work
  • They'll find "hidden" problems during the job and hit you with change orders
  • They may not be insured or licensed

Roofing quality directly affects how long your roof lasts. Cutting corners to save a few hundred dollars now can cost you thousands in premature replacement or damage.

Demands Full Payment Upfront

Legitimate contractors ask for a deposit (typically 25-50%) to purchase materials, with balance due upon completion or in progress payments. A contractor demanding 100% upfront is a serious red flag. You have no guarantee the work will be done, and if something goes wrong, recovering your money is difficult.

No License or Insurance

Ask to see the contractor's license and proof of liability and workers' compensation insurance. Verify the license number with Connecticut's Department of Consumer Protection. If they can't show these documents, don't hire them. Unlicensed contractors may do poor work, and if someone is injured on your property, you could face liability.

Vague Warranty Terms

Warranties should specify:

  • What's covered: Materials, labor, or both?
  • How long: Usually 5-10 years for workmanship, 25-30 years for materials
  • What voids it: Some warranties exclude damage from extreme weather, animal damage, or improper maintenance
  • How to file a claim: Process and timeline

If the estimate doesn't include clear warranty terms, ask for them in writing before signing.

Doesn't Include Permits

Building permits are required in most Connecticut towns for roof replacement. A contractor who doesn't include permit costs might be planning to skip the permit process. This is illegal and can cause problems if you sell your home (unpermitted work must be disclosed). Legitimate contractors always pull permits.

No Mention of Ventilation or Ice Shield

These are essential in Connecticut's climate. A roof without proper ventilation will fail prematurely. Ice shield prevents ice dam damage. If an estimate doesn't mention these, either ask to have them added or hire a different contractor. These aren't optional upgrades—they're necessary components of a quality roof.

Pushes You to Decide Immediately

High-pressure sales tactics ("This price is only good today" or "I can squeeze you in this week if you decide now") are red flags. Professional contractors are busy but don't rush homeowners into decisions. Take time to compare estimates and think about your options.

Questions to Ask Every Contractor

  1. How long have you been in business? (Look for 5+ years of experience)
  2. Can you provide references? (Call at least three)
  3. Are you licensed and insured? (Verify both)
  4. What warranty do you provide on your workmanship? (Get it in writing)
  5. Will you pull permits? (Should always say yes)
  6. What happens if you find hidden damage during the project? (How are additional costs handled?)
  7. Who handles the roof once it's done? (You should be able to contact them about warranty issues)
  8. Do you recommend ice shield and ventilation upgrades? (Yes is the right answer for Connecticut)

Comparing Estimates: Don't Just Look at Price

When comparing three estimates, don't automatically choose the cheapest. Consider:

  • Are all estimates using the same materials (same brand and quality of shingles)?
  • Are all estimates including the same scope (ventilation, ice shield, flashing, permits)?
  • Is the contractor licensed, insured, and established?
  • Did they listen to your concerns and answer questions thoroughly?
  • What's their warranty coverage?

A $12,000 roof from an established, licensed contractor is a better investment than a $9,000 roof from an unlicensed contractor who cuts corners.

Final Checklist Before You Sign

  • Estimate is detailed with specific materials and labor itemized
  • Contractor is licensed and insured (verify both)
  • Permits are included in the cost
  • Ventilation and ice shield are included
  • Warranty terms are clear and in writing
  • Payment schedule is reasonable (no full payment upfront)
  • Timeline is clearly stated
  • You've checked references from past jobs
  • You've obtained at least two estimates to compare

A good roofing estimate takes time to prepare. If a contractor gives you a one-page estimate without detail, that's a sign they don't take their work seriously. Professional contractors provide detailed, transparent estimates because they're confident in their pricing and quality. Use that confidence to guide your decision.
