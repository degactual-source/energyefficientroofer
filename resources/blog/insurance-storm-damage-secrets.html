What Connecticut Homeowners Don't Know About Storm Damage Insurance Claims

After managing more than $2 million in active Connecticut storm damage insurance claims, I have sat across from adjusters on hundreds of roofs. The adjuster's job is to settle the claim accurately — but "accurately" is a matter of what gets documented, what gets supplemented, and how well the homeowner understands the process. These are the seven things adjusters don't volunteer, that most CT homeowners never learn until it's too late to recover the money.

Secret 1: The Initial Adjuster Estimate Is a Starting Point, Not the Final Number

The first estimate your insurance company sends after a storm damage claim is not a final settlement offer. It's the adjuster's initial assessment of what they observed, valued at Xactimate pricing (a software database that insurance companies use to set cost standards). That number is routinely incomplete — not necessarily because the adjuster is acting in bad faith, but because adjusters work fast, cover many properties after a storm event, and don't always catch every line item.

The supplement process exists specifically to address this. A supplement is a formal request to add scope or price items that were missed in the initial estimate. Common items supplemented on CT storm damage claims include: code upgrade items (drip edge, ice and water shield), overhead and profit for general contractor coordination, updated material pricing when the Xactimate database is behind current market costs, and storm-damaged components that weren't physically accessible during the initial inspection (like decking damage that only becomes visible at tear-off).

Energy Efficient Roofer reviews every adjuster estimate against our own scope before presenting it to you. If items are missing, we submit the supplement with documentation — photos, measurements, code references — before work begins. The supplement is your money. Don't leave it uncollected.

Secret 2: Soft Metals Are the Easiest Proof of Hail — Check Them Before the Adjuster Arrives

Hail damage to asphalt shingles is sometimes subtle and genuinely disputable. Hail damage to soft metals — gutters, step flashing, chimney cap, AC condenser fins, downspout bands, ridge vent caps — is unmistakable. Hail leaves round, uniform spatter marks on soft metals that don't look like any other kind of impact. They're also permanent: unlike granule displacement on shingles, metal spatter marks don't fade or recover.

Before your adjuster arrives, document all the soft metals on your property. Walk the perimeter with your phone camera. Check your gutters — run your hand along the inside of the gutter and look for round dents on the face. Check the step flashing at the sides of any chimney or dormer. Check your AC unit's condenser fins. Check any skylights for frame damage. This documentation gives you contemporaneous evidence of the hail event that exists independently of your adjuster's opinion about shingle damage.

If your adjuster denies hail damage to the roof but your gutters clearly show uniform spatter marks, you have grounds for re-inspection. Soft metal documentation has won claims that adjuster shingle assessments initially denied.

Secret 3: RCV Policies Have a "Holdback" — You Don't Get Paid in Full Until the Work Is Done

Most Connecticut homeowner policies are RCV (Replacement Cost Value) policies, which means they pay the full cost to replace your damaged property with new materials of like kind and quality. What many homeowners don't realize: RCV policies pay in two installments.

The first check is the ACV — Actual Cash Value — which is the replacement cost minus depreciation based on the age and condition of the damaged property. For a 15-year-old roof, that might mean the first check represents 50–60% of the total replacement cost. The second payment — the "holdback" or "recoverable depreciation" — is released only after you complete the repair and submit the contractor's invoice as proof.

Homeowners who cash the first ACV check and don't do the work forfeit the holdback — often thousands of dollars. And their roof is still damaged. The only way to collect the full RCV settlement is to complete the work with a licensed contractor, submit the invoice to the carrier, and formally request the holdback release. That's a step we walk every Energy Efficient Roofer client through as part of the claim process.

Secret 4: You Can Dispute the First Decision

A first denial or underpayment is not the end of the claim. In Connecticut, homeowners have several options for disputing a storm damage determination:

Re-inspection request: You can request that the carrier send a different adjuster for a second look, particularly if you have additional documentation (soft metal photos, a contractor's written assessment, weather data showing hail or wind at your address on the event date). Having a contractor present at the re-inspection to walk the roof with the adjuster and point out damage is often the difference between approval and denial.

Appraisal clause: Most Connecticut homeowner policies contain an appraisal clause that allows either party to demand a binding appraisal process. Under appraisal, each party selects a qualified appraiser, and if they don't agree, an umpire makes the final determination. This process is frequently used in CT storm damage disputes and often produces a settlement significantly higher than the initial adjuster estimate — particularly when the dispute involves judgment calls about the extent of hail damage or the replacement of matching materials.

Connecticut Insurance Department: If you believe your carrier is acting in bad faith — ignoring documentation, stalling payment, misrepresenting policy terms — you can file a complaint with the Connecticut Insurance Department. This is a consumer protection process, and it gets results in clear-cut cases of carrier misconduct.

Secret 5: "Depreciation" Is Not "Denial" — Recoverable Depreciation Is Your Money

One of the most common points of confusion in CT insurance claims: homeowners receive an ACV estimate and assume the carrier is saying that's all they'll pay. It's not. The ACV is the floor, not the ceiling.

Under an RCV policy, the depreciated amount is "recoverable" — meaning it's held in trust until the work is done, then paid out. If your adjuster estimate shows $18,000 replacement cost, $5,400 depreciation, and a $2,000 deductible, your first check is approximately $10,600. But the $5,400 in depreciation comes back to you after the work is complete and invoiced. Your net out-of-pocket on that claim is just the $2,000 deductible — not $7,400.

I see homeowners look at the first ACV check, compare it to contractor estimates, and conclude the claim isn't worth pursuing. That math is wrong because it ignores the holdback. Every RCV claim should be pursued to completion, with the supplement submitted, the work done, and the holdback collected.

Secret 6: Code Upgrades Are Often Covered — and Most CT Homeowners Leave Them on the Table

Connecticut building code has been updated several times in the last 20 years. Many older homes were built and roofed when less stringent standards applied — no drip edge required, less ice and water shield coverage required, lower ventilation ratios. When you replace a storm-damaged roof today, the new installation must meet current code — and the cost of upgrading to current code is typically covered under your policy's "ordinance or law" or "code upgrade" provision.

Code upgrade items in a typical CT roof replacement include:

  • Drip edge — required along all eaves and rakes under current CT residential code
  • Ice and water shield — extended coverage at eaves, valleys, penetrations, and all low-slope sections
  • Ventilation upgrades — if the existing system doesn't meet current net-free-area minimums, the cost to bring it into compliance is a code upgrade item
  • Permit fee — the building permit required for the replacement
  • Deck replacement — if tear-off reveals rot or deterioration that must be replaced before new shingles are installed

These items are routinely omitted from the initial adjuster estimate because the adjuster is pricing the shingle replacement, not performing a code compliance audit. Submitting these as supplement line items — with specific code references — is standard practice and is not an adversarial act. It's proper documentation of what the replacement legally requires.

Secret 7: The Statute of Limitations Runs — File Within the Policy Period

Connecticut homeowner insurance policies have time limits. Most require that you report damage promptly (typically within 60 days of the event) and that you complete the repair within a defined period (often one to two years). Beyond those policy deadlines, most CT policies and general statutes of limitations impose a two-year window for legal action from the date of loss.

Storm damage from a significant hail event in June can be dismissed as "pre-existing wear" by an adjuster if you don't file until the following spring. The contemporaneous documentation matters — weather data on the event date, photos taken within days of the storm, contractor notes written at the time of inspection. Waiting to see if the damage gets worse is not a strategy that helps your claim.

If you experienced a significant storm — hail, wind, ice dam — and have not had your roof inspected, do it now. The inspection is free. Documenting the damage contemporaneously protects your claim. And if damage exists, waiting only gives the carrier more grounds to argue that whatever they see at inspection reflects gradual wear rather than a discrete storm event.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do I have to file a storm damage claim in Connecticut?

In Connecticut, homeowner insurance policies typically require prompt reporting — most have a 60-day reporting requirement, with a longer period to complete repairs. The statute of limitations for a lawsuit arising from an insurance dispute in Connecticut is generally two years from the date of loss under most policies. Do not delay filing. If a significant storm hit and you haven't had your roof inspected, call a contractor to document the damage now.

What is an RCV policy and how does the holdback work?

An RCV (Replacement Cost Value) policy pays the full cost to replace damaged property with new materials of like kind and quality. The carrier pays first at ACV (depreciated value), then releases the holdback — the depreciation — after you complete the work and submit the invoice. You must actually complete the repair to collect the holdback. Pocketing the ACV check and skipping the work means leaving recoverable money on the table.

What are code upgrade items in a roof insurance claim?

Code upgrade items pay for bringing the replacement into compliance with current building codes — even if the original structure was built before those codes existed. In CT roofing, the most common code upgrade items are: drip edge, extended ice and water shield, upgraded ventilation to meet current net-free-area requirements, and the building permit. These are routinely omitted from initial adjuster estimates and submitted as supplement line items.

Can I dispute a denied or underpaid storm damage claim in Connecticut?

Yes. A first determination is not final. Options include: requesting a re-inspection with a contractor present, invoking the appraisal clause in your policy (most CT homeowner policies contain one), or filing a complaint with the Connecticut Insurance Department. The appraisal process is frequently used in CT storm damage disputes and often produces a higher settlement than the initial adjuster estimate.
