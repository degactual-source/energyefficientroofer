What Your Insurance Company Won't Tell You About Storm Damage Claims

Insurance March 10, 2026 7 min read

Storm season is here. When a nor'easter tears shingles off your roof or hail pounds your siding, you'll file an insurance claim expecting a quick resolution and a fair payout. But insurance companies have a well-documented playbook for settling claims—and it doesn't always work in your favor. Here's what they won't volunteer and what you need to know to protect yourself.

Secret #1: The Adjuster Works for the Insurance Company, Not You

This is the most important secret, and it fundamentally shapes how claims are handled. The insurance adjuster is an employee or contractor hired by your insurance company. Their job is to minimize claims payouts. They are not impartial, and they are not there to help you maximize your claim.

This doesn't mean they're dishonest—many adjusters do their job professionally and fairly. But their incentive structure favors the insurance company. A low estimate saves the company money. A high estimate costs them. You're the only party in the room without professional representation, and that puts you at a disadvantage.

Insurance companies count on homeowners not knowing this, not questioning estimates, and not hiring independent professionals to verify damage. Don't be that homeowner.

Secret #2: Initial Estimates Are Often Deliberately Low

The first estimate from the insurance adjuster is frequently lower than the actual cost of repairs. This is standard practice. The strategy is simple: provide a number that seems acceptable, hope the homeowner accepts it without question, and move on.

How low? We've seen estimates that miss entire categories of damage—like underlying rot, structural issues, or necessary protective measures. Insurance companies know that many homeowners will accept the first number rather than push back. For a company processing thousands of claims, even a small average reduction per claim adds up to millions in savings.

The good news: you don't have to accept their estimate. You have the right to get your own.

Secret #3: You Have the Right to Your Own Estimate

This is where homeowners take control. Connecticut law (and most state insurance laws) allows you to hire an independent contractor to assess the damage and provide an estimate. This is called an "independent adjuster" or in the roofing industry, simply a professional estimate.

If your independent estimate differs significantly from the insurance company's estimate, you can file a "supplement claim" (more on that below). You're not limited to what the insurance company initially determines.

Important: hire a licensed, insured contractor with no financial incentive to inflate costs. A good contractor will provide an honest assessment, not just a high number to fight the insurance company. Their credibility matters in disputes.

Secret #4: The Supplement Process Exists for a Reason

When your independent estimate reveals damage the insurance company missed, you file a supplement claim. This is a formal request for additional payment to cover the additional damages discovered.

Insurance companies know supplement claims happen. In fact, they count on most homeowners not filing them. If your initial estimate was $8,000 but the actual cost is $12,000 (due to hidden rot, structural damage, or code compliance issues), a supplement claim recovers that gap.

Here's the process: you provide documentation (contractor estimates, photos, detailed scope) supporting the additional damage. The insurance company either agrees and issues a supplement, or they dispute it and send their own adjuster for a second opinion. From here, you might enter a dispute resolution process.

The key: file supplements promptly. Most policies have time limits (usually 60-90 days from the initial loss). Don't sit on evidence of additional damage.

Secret #5: Depreciation vs. Replacement Cost Is a Trap

Here's where insurance companies save the most money: depreciation. If your policy has a depreciation clause (common in older policies), the insurance company pays the current value of materials, not the cost to replace them with new materials.

Example: Your roof has a 25-year lifespan and is 15 years old. The insurance company might depreciate it 60%. If replacement cost is $12,000, they deduct depreciation and pay you $4,800. You then have to pay the additional $7,200 out of pocket to replace the roof.

This is particularly unfair because a roof is often damaged by an event (storm, hail) that's not your fault, yet you bear the cost of your roof's age. Many newer policies include "replacement cost value" (RCV) instead, which pays for new materials without depreciation. If your policy still has depreciation, ask your agent about upgrading.

What to check: Review your policy documents and look for "replacement cost" vs. "actual cash value." Better yet, call your agent and ask directly what your roof is covered under. If it's depreciated, consider changing policies before storm season.

Secret #6: Code Upgrade Coverage Is Limited (But It Exists)

Building codes change. When you repair damage, modern building codes may require upgrades your original installation didn't have. Examples include enhanced ice shield requirements in Connecticut (critical for freeze-thaw climates), upgraded ventilation, or electrical/plumbing codes.

Insurance companies often resist paying for code upgrades, arguing that the homeowner should have updated things originally. But many policies include "code upgrade coverage" up to a percentage of the claim value.

If your roofer identifies code requirements necessary for a proper repair, document it. These upgrades are often essential (ice shield is not optional in Connecticut) and are frequently covered under code upgrade provisions.

Secret #7: There Are Time Limits on Filing Claims

Most insurance policies require you to report damage within 30 days of discovery. Missed that window? The insurance company can deny the claim, even if the damage was clearly caused by the insured event.

Don't delay. After a storm, get a professional inspection quickly. Document damage with photos. File your claim within the policy timeline. Delays give insurance companies leverage to deny claims or reduce payouts.

Secret #8: Undisclosed Damage Comes Back to Haunt You

If your contractor discovers damage during repair that wasn't in the original claim, you can file a supplement, but only if you discover it during the repair process. Once the claim is closed and paid, new damage found later may be your responsibility.

This is why a thorough inspection by a qualified contractor before you accept the insurance company's estimate is critical. A good contractor will flag potential hidden damage (rot, structural issues, inadequate ventilation) during their initial assessment.

Secret #9: Documentation Is Everything

Insurance companies will challenge vague or poorly documented claims. When you file:

  • Take clear photos and videos of all damage from multiple angles
  • Include wide shots showing the overall damage context
  • Get written estimates from licensed contractors
  • Keep all correspondence with the insurance company in writing (email, not phone calls)
  • Document the date and nature of the storm
  • Keep receipts for temporary repairs or mitigation measures

Professional documentation strengthens your position in disputes and makes supplement claims easier to justify.

Secret #10: You Have Recourse if the Claim Is Unfair

If you believe the insurance company is acting in bad faith—denying a legitimate claim, refusing to cover disclosed damage, or misapplying policy terms—you have options:

  • Demand review: Formally request that the company re-examine the claim with your documentation.
  • File a complaint: Connecticut's Insurance Department investigates complaints against insurers. If they find issues, it becomes leverage in negotiations.
  • Hire a public adjuster or attorney: For large claims, hiring a professional advocate can increase your recovery significantly. They take a percentage of the additional payout (usually 10-15%) but often negotiate settlements far exceeding their fee.
  • Demand appraisal: Some policies include an appraisal clause allowing either party to demand an independent appraisal if the estimate is disputed. This can resolve high-stakes disagreements.

The Bottom Line: Be Proactive

Insurance companies aren't villains—they're businesses managing risk and claims efficiently. But their efficiency bias works against homeowners who don't know the system. Here's your action plan:

  1. Report damage immediately after a storm.
  2. Hire an independent contractor to assess damage thoroughly.
  3. Get detailed, itemized estimates.
  4. Compare to the insurance estimate and look for gaps.
  5. File supplements for any discovered additional damage.
  6. Know your coverage (depreciation vs. RCV, code upgrades).
  7. Document everything in writing.
  8. Don't accept lowball estimates without pushback.

A fair insurance settlement takes work, but it's work worth doing. You paid your premiums in good faith. The insurance company should honor their obligation fairly.
