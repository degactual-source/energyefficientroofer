What storm damage is covered under a CT homeowner's insurance policy?

Connecticut homeowners carry policies that generally cover sudden and accidental damage caused by named perils. For roofs, the most common covered events are wind, hail, ice dams, and falling objects. What your policy does not cover is equally important to understand — and adjusters rarely volunteer that information clearly.

Wind damage

Wind is the most common storm damage claim in Connecticut. Covered wind damage includes shingles that have been blown completely off or lifted at their seal tab, ridge cap sections that have separated, step and counter-flashing that has been pulled away from walls or chimneys, and drip edge metal that has lifted. Connecticut gets both nor'easters — which can sustain winds over 50 mph for hours at a time — and tropical remnants that bring shorter but more intense gusts. Fairfield County, sitting at the coastline, sees some of the highest sustained winds in the state.

What adjusters sometimes try to exclude: a single lifted shingle tab, calling it "cosmetic" rather than functional damage. A properly written supplement will include waterproofing verification and address whether the roof seal integrity has been compromised — which it has whenever wind has broken the factory-applied sealant.

Hail damage

Hail is the most disputed storm damage category in Connecticut. Carriers frequently attempt to deny hail claims by arguing that visible granule loss is "normal aging" rather than hail impact. This argument is used most aggressively when the hail event was marginal — stones smaller than one inch in diameter. In Fairfield and New Haven counties, there is a documented hail corridor where late-spring and early-summer supercell thunderstorms track northeast along Long Island Sound. Multiple major hail events in recent years have generated thousands of valid claims that were initially denied as wear and tear.

What is actually covered: granule displacement in random spatter patterns across the field of the roof, cracked shingles, impact fractures in the mat below granule loss, and dented or dimpled soft metal surfaces. Hail damage is not a straight-line pattern. It is scattered, random, and appears across the entire exposed field.

Ice dam damage

An ice dam forms when heat escaping through an under-insulated or under-ventilated attic melts snow on the upper portion of the roof. That meltwater flows down to the cold roof overhang, refreezes, and builds a ridge of ice. As the cycle continues, backed-up water has nowhere to go except under the shingles, through the decking, and into the living space below. CT winters are perfectly calibrated to produce ice dams: alternating freeze-thaw cycles, frequent overnight temperatures in the teens, and significant snowfall events.

Interior water damage resulting from ice dams is covered under most CT homeowner policies as a sudden and accidental loss. What is not covered is the ice dam removal itself or any preventive measures. The interior damage — stained ceilings, ruined insulation, damaged drywall — is the covered portion of the claim.

Falling objects

Trees, branches, and debris falling onto a roof are covered under most CT policies as falling object damage. This is typically straightforward — the damage is visible and localized — though carriers will try to limit the scope to the immediately impacted area rather than the full structural compromise that a significant strike can cause.

What is NOT covered

Normal wear and tear is explicitly excluded from every standard homeowner policy. A 25-year-old roof with curled, cracked, and alligatored shingles that is leaking because it has reached the end of its service life is not an insurance claim — it is a maintenance issue. Manufacturing defects are similarly excluded from homeowner policies and must be pursued separately through the manufacturer. Neglected maintenance — a known flashing failure that was left unaddressed for years, resulting in rot — is also excluded. Adjusters use this language aggressively, and one of EER's most important roles is to document the distinction between pre-existing wear and new storm damage, particularly when the same roof has both conditions.

How do I know if I have hail damage on my roof in CT?

Most homeowners cannot tell the difference between real hail damage and normal granule loss from an aging roof. Insurance adjusters know this, and some use homeowner uncertainty to their advantage. Here is exactly what to look for — on the roof itself and on the soft metal surfaces around your home.

The spatter pattern test

The most reliable indicator of genuine hail impact is the random, scattered pattern of damage across the entire field of the roof. Hail falls on every exposed surface simultaneously. On an asphalt shingle, impact marks appear as circular or slightly oblong areas where granules have been knocked loose, exposing the darker asphalt mat below. Critically, these marks are random — not concentrated at wear points, not following water drainage patterns, not limited to south-facing slopes. If you see a consistent scatter pattern across the whole field, you have hail damage. If granule loss is concentrated at the edges, valleys, or ridges only, that is more consistent with normal wear.

Check soft metals first

Before you ever get on the roof, check the soft metal surfaces around your home. Aluminum step flashing at sidewall intersections, valley metal, gutter aprons, and downspout elbows are all excellent hail impact indicators. A hail stone that leaves no visible mark on a hard asphalt shingle will leave a clear, circular dent in soft aluminum flashing. Look for a field of random dimples. Check the fins on your HVAC condenser unit in the backyard — even a modest hail event will dimple aluminum fins. Check window screens for impact tears or distortion. Check the garage door panels. These are all "ground truth" confirmations of a genuine hail event that support the roof claim.

Hail size and carrier thresholds

In Connecticut, most carriers use a functional damage standard for hail claims. The general threshold is one inch diameter — approximately marble-size — for insurers to approve a full replacement. Sub-inch hail can still cause damage, but carriers will dispute it more aggressively. Official hail size records are pulled from NOAA's Storm Events Database for the specific date and zip code. Mike pulls NOAA documentation on every claim as a baseline for the supplement file.

What happens during a roof insurance inspection in Connecticut?

The insurance adjuster process is a negotiation disguised as an inspection. Understanding each step — and what EER does at each step — is the difference between a claim that pays the full replacement cost and one that pays half.

Step 1: File the claim

After damage is documented, you call your carrier's claims line and open a claim. The carrier assigns either a staff adjuster (employed by the carrier) or an independent adjuster (contracted by the carrier). Both are paid by the insurance company. Neither is on your side, though good adjusters are professionals who write fair estimates when a contractor holds them accountable.

Step 2: EER meets the adjuster on-site

Mike Demo attends every adjuster meeting at every property. This is non-negotiable at EER. The adjuster's estimate is largely shaped by what the contractor points out during the inspection. An adjuster walking a roof alone will note the obvious damage; Mike ensures they note every code-required upgrade, every accessory item, every flashing component, and every area of functional compromise. Being present at the adjuster meeting is worth thousands of dollars on the average CT claim.

Steps 3 through 5: Estimate, supplement, negotiation

The adjuster submits an initial estimate to the carrier. EER reviews that estimate line by line against the actual scope of work required to complete the project to CT building code. Items that are missing, underpriced, or not covered by Xactimate's area measurements are compiled into a written supplement. EER submits the supplement to the carrier's claims desk with supporting documentation. Most carriers respond within five to ten business days. Some require a re-inspection; others approve the supplement by desk review.

Steps 6 through 10: Approval, construction, holdback

Once the revised estimate is approved, EER schedules the work. During tear-off, if decking damage or additional structural compromise is discovered that was not visible during the initial inspection, a final supplement is submitted before that hidden scope is closed in. After the project is complete, EER submits a completion certificate and final invoice. The carrier then releases the RCV holdback — the depreciation amount that was withheld pending completion of the replacement.

What is a roof insurance supplement and why do most claims need one?

A supplement is an addendum to the insurance carrier's initial estimate that adds line items, corrects measurements, or adjusts unit pricing to reflect the actual cost of properly completing the approved scope of work. In Mike Demo's experience working CT claims for 25 years, the vast majority of initial adjuster estimates are incomplete — not necessarily because of bad faith, but because adjusters are writing estimates rapidly across dozens of claims after a major storm event.

What adjusters commonly miss on CT roof claims

Drip edge and eave metal — often omitted entirely from the estimate even though CT building code requires it on a full replacement. Ice and water shield — CT code requires ice and water shield at eaves, valleys, and penetrations. Many adjuster estimates include only felt underlayment, which is insufficient under code. Synthetic underlayment — the industry has moved from felt to synthetic, but Xactimate's default assumptions in some regions still price felt. Ridge cap shingles — separate line item from field shingles; frequently underquantified. Pipe boot flashings — every roof penetration requires a replacement boot; adjusters often omit several. Chimney counter-flashing — step and counter-flashing are separate items; estimates often lump them or omit counter-flashing entirely. Permit fee — most CT municipalities require a building permit for a roof replacement. This is a real project cost that is payable from the insurance proceeds. Dumpster haul-away — debris removal is a project cost; many estimates underprice it based on regional Xactimate defaults that have not been updated to reflect current disposal costs. Code upgrade items — when building code requires something new that was not on the original roof (drip edge where none existed, ice and water shield where only felt was present), those code-mandated upgrades are generally covered under the policy's code upgrade or law and ordinance provisions.

What is Actual Cash Value (ACV) vs. Replacement Cost Value (RCV) and which should I have?

This is one of the most consequential distinctions in CT homeowner insurance, and one that many policyholders do not understand until they file a claim.

Actual Cash Value (ACV)

An ACV policy pays you the current depreciated value of the damaged item. A 20-year-old architectural shingle roof that costs $18,000 to replace might be depreciated at 60%, leaving you with an ACV payout of $7,200 minus your deductible. You cover the remaining $10,800 out of pocket. ACV policies are cheaper in premium, but they leave homeowners substantially short when they need to file a major claim.

Replacement Cost Value (RCV)

An RCV policy pays the full cost to replace the damaged item with new materials of like kind and quality, without applying depreciation. Most CT homeowner policies are RCV policies, but the RCV payment is structured as a two-part disbursement. The carrier pays the ACV amount upfront (depreciation withheld as a "holdback"), and releases the holdback only after the replacement work is completed and documented. This structure is designed to ensure the homeowner actually replaces the roof rather than pocketing the money and leaving the house unprotected.

Recovering the RCV holdback

To collect the depreciation holdback, EER submits a completion certificate and final invoice to the carrier after the project is finished. The carrier then issues the second payment. This process typically takes 1 to 3 weeks. Some carriers delay; Mike follows up directly with the claims desk when holdback release is slow, as there are CT Insurance Department regulations governing timely claims payment.

What if the insurance carrier denies my storm damage claim in CT?

Claim denials are not the end of the road. They are the beginning of a process — and EER has experience navigating every step of it.

Common denial reasons and how to fight them

The two most common denial grounds are "normal wear and tear" and "pre-existing damage." Both are genuinely applicable in some cases, and both are over-applied by carriers in others. If EER documents a clear spatter pattern of hail impacts across a field of shingles that also shows some normal aging, the aging does not eliminate coverage for the new hail damage. Each event is evaluated on its own merits.

The first step after a denial is to request a re-inspection with EER present. If the original inspection was conducted without the contractor present, a second inspection with Mike there can produce a different outcome. EER also documents neighboring properties with similar damage and pulls storm event records from NOAA and the Insurance Services Office (ISO) to establish that the storm was real, severe, and caused documented damage across the same storm track.

The appraisal clause

Most CT homeowner policies contain an appraisal clause: a binding dispute resolution mechanism that does not require litigation. If you and the carrier cannot agree on the amount of loss, either party can invoke appraisal. Each side hires an independent appraiser; the two appraisers select a neutral umpire; the umpire's decision (agreeing with either appraiser) is binding on both parties. For valid claims that have been seriously underpaid, the appraisal process often resolves the dispute faster and cheaper than litigation.

CT Insurance Department complaint

The Connecticut Insurance Department (CID) regulates carrier claims handling practices under CT Gen. Stat. § 38a-816. If a carrier is engaging in unfair claims settlement practices — denying without adequate investigation, unreasonably delaying payment, misrepresenting policy provisions — a complaint to the CID creates a formal regulatory record and often accelerates resolution. The CID complaint process is free.

What is the CT statute of limitations on a storm damage insurance claim?

This is one of the most dangerous areas of CT homeowner insurance, and it catches more homeowners than any other issue Mike has seen in 25 years of working claims.

Most CT homeowner policies require you to provide notice of a loss "as soon as reasonably practicable" after you become aware of it, and to file a formal proof of loss within a timeframe specified in the policy — often 60 days for the formal proof, but the claim filing window itself may run one to two years from the date of loss. Once that window closes, the carrier can deny the claim as untimely regardless of how valid the underlying damage is.

The practical problem in CT is that storm damage — particularly hail damage — is not always visible from the ground and may not cause an obvious leak for one or two years after the event. A hail strike damages the mat of the shingle, accelerating granule loss and reducing the shingle's water-shedding effectiveness, but the roof may not leak until the next severe rain event. By the time a homeowner notices a leak and connects it to a hail event from eighteen months prior, the claim window may have closed.

If you suspect your roof sustained storm damage in the last one to two years, call EER for an inspection now. Do not wait until you see water inside the house. NOAA's storm database can be searched by date and zip code to identify documented hail and wind events for your area — Mike pulls this data for every inspection to establish whether a documented storm event occurred within your claim window.

Frequently asked questions about CT storm damage insurance claims

Does it cost anything to have Energy Efficient Roofer handle my insurance claim?

EER charges nothing above the approved insurance scope for storm damage work covered by the claim. You pay your deductible — that is it in most cases. EER earns its margin by writing a thorough supplement that captures every legitimate line item, not by charging homeowners anything extra.

Can I use any contractor for my CT roof insurance claim?

Yes. You are not required to use your insurer's preferred contractor. Connecticut law protects your right to choose your own contractor. Using an independent contractor who will write a proper supplement often results in a larger approved scope and a better-quality completed project than using whoever the carrier recommends. Preferred contractor programs are designed to lower the carrier's cost — not to maximize your claim.

How long does a roof insurance claim take in Connecticut?

A straightforward CT roof claim typically moves from inspection to approval in two to six weeks. Supplement disputes can add two to four weeks. Mike's experience working with Travelers, Allstate, State Farm, Liberty Mutual, and Amica — the most common carriers in Fairfield County — helps move things faster because he understands each carrier's internal review and escalation process.

What is an ice dam and does it make a valid insurance claim?

An ice dam is a ridge of ice that forms at the roof eave, blocking melt water from draining off the roof and forcing it under shingles into the attic and walls. Interior water damage from ice dams is covered under most CT homeowner policies as sudden and accidental water damage. The coverage applies to the resulting interior damage — stained ceilings, ruined insulation, damaged drywall — not to the ice dam removal or the underlying ventilation issue that caused it.

Should I file a homeowner claim for a small roof repair in CT?

Generally no for small repairs under $2,000 — the claim may raise your premiums more than the payout is worth, and some CT carriers will non-renew a policy after multiple small claims. For whole-roof damage from a named storm or a documented hail event affecting the entire roof field, always file. Call EER for an honest assessment before you decide.

What is the CT consumer protection law for roofing contractors and insurance claims?

Connecticut regulations prohibit contractors from waiving a homeowner's deductible. Offering to absorb your deductible is considered insurance fraud under CT law — it exposes both you and the contractor to legal liability. Legitimate contractors like EER work within the approved insurance scope: you pay the deductible, the carrier pays the rest.

What carriers does EER have experience with for CT storm claims?

Mike Demo has worked claims with Travelers, State Farm, Allstate, Amica, Liberty Mutual, USAA, Nationwide, Chubb, and many regional CT carriers. Each carrier has different adjuster protocols, supplement formats, and internal escalation paths. That carrier-specific knowledge is a material advantage in supplement negotiations.

What documentation do I need for a storm damage claim in Connecticut?

The date of the storm, any photos taken immediately after, and previous roof documentation if you have it. EER handles all current damage documentation with professional photographs and a written assessment. NOAA storm data reports are pulled from the National Centers for Environmental Information database — this provides official documentation of wind speed, hail size, and storm track that is critical in supplement negotiations and denial appeals.
