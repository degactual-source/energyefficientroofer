What to Do Immediately After Storm Damage

⚠️ SAFETY FIRST

Before documenting anything, ensure your family is safe. Turn off electricity if water is present. Do not enter attic spaces or damaged areas until safety is confirmed.

The Critical First 72 Hours

Step 1: Ensure Safety & Prevent Further Damage (First 1 Hour)

  • Stop water intrusion: Use tarps to cover damaged roof sections, block holes, and prevent interior water damage. This is critical—water damage claims exceed structural damage claims by 3:1.
  • Move valuables: Move furniture, electronics, and irreplaceable items away from damaged areas.
  • Shut off electricity: If water is present, shut off power to affected areas to prevent electrical hazards.
  • Document initial damage: Take photos and video of visible damage before mitigation efforts. These images protect your claim.

Step 2: Document Damage Comprehensively (Hours 1-24)

  • Photos and video: Photograph every damaged area from multiple angles. Include close-ups and wide shots. Video walkthrough of the entire affected area.
  • Interior damage: Document water intrusion, staining, damaged drywall, ruined insulation, and affected belongings.
  • Write detailed notes: Document the storm date, wind speed (if known), hail size, and specific damage locations. Include timestamps.
  • Save weather records: Screenshot weather service records confirming the storm date and intensity. These prove the cause of damage.
  • Create a damage inventory: List all damaged items with replacement costs. This supports your claim value.

Step 3: Call Your Insurance Company (Within 24 Hours)

  • Do NOT delay: Most insurance policies require prompt notification. Delays can jeopardize your claim.
  • Provide specific details: Tell them the date of the storm, type of damage (hail, wind, water), and affected areas.
  • Take notes: Write down the claim number, adjuster's name, and expected inspection date.
  • Email confirmation: Follow up your phone call with an email summarizing what you reported.
  • Do NOT accept payment yet: Don't sign anything or accept initial payment offers until you understand the full scope of damage.

Step 4: DO NOT Sign Anything—Especially Storm Chasers (Critical!)

How the Insurance Claims Process Actually Works

Understanding the 6-Step Insurance Claims Journey

Step 1: Filing the Claim

You initiate the claim by contacting your insurance company within 24-48 hours of discovering damage. You provide basic information: date of damage, type of damage, affected areas. The insurer assigns a claim number and schedules an adjuster inspection.

Timeline: Same day to 24 hours

Step 2: Adjuster Inspection

An insurance company adjuster visits your property to assess damage. This is NOT an independent assessment—the adjuster works for the insurance company and is incentivized to keep payouts low. The adjuster:

  • Inspects visible damage (but may miss underlying damage)
  • Takes photos and measurements
  • Estimates repair costs using their own pricing
  • Applies depreciation calculations (older roofs get reduced payouts)
  • Issues an initial estimate

Timeline: 3-14 days after claim filing

Your action: Be present during the inspection. Take your own photos. Document everything the adjuster observes and skips.

Step 3: Initial Estimate & Scope of Work

The insurance company provides a detailed estimate showing the damage, repair items, and cost breakdown. However, initial estimates are typically:

  • 30-50% lower than actual repair costs
  • Missing structural damage and secondary damage (water intrusion, insulation damage)
  • Underestimating code upgrade costs
  • Applying excessive depreciation
  • Using outdated pricing or labor rates

Timeline: 7-21 days after adjuster visit

Critical insight: This initial estimate is NOT final. You have the right to dispute it, request supplements, and obtain independent estimates.

Step 4: Supplement Claims (Where the Real Work Happens)

A supplement is a formal request to increase the insurance company's payment to cover additional or undercalculated damage. This is where most homeowners either get their full coverage or leave money on the table.

Supplements can cover:

  • Structural damage missed in the initial inspection
  • Water intrusion and secondary damage
  • Modern building code upgrades (foundation mounting, hurricane straps, ventilation improvements)
  • Corrected labor rates and material costs
  • Manufacturer specification compliance costs
  • Removal and disposal of old materials

Timeline: 14-45 days after initial estimate

Step 5: Insurance Approval

The insurance company reviews your supplement claim and either approves it, partially approves it, or denies it. If approved, the claim amount increases. You may need multiple supplement rounds—some claims require 2-4 supplements to reach full coverage.

Timeline: 7-30 days per supplement review

Step 6: Payment & Repair

Once the claim is approved, you receive payment (typically 50-100% of approved amount, with remainder upon completion inspection). You can then hire a contractor and begin repairs.

Timeline: 30-90 days for repairs after approval

What Most Homeowners Don't Know (But Insurance Companies Hope You Never Learn)

Critical Insurance Secrets That Could Cost You Thousands

1. Insurance Adjusters Work for the Insurance Company, Not You

This is the #1 misunderstanding homeowners have. When an insurance company sends an adjuster to your property, that adjuster is an employee (or contracted by the insurer) and is incentivized to keep payouts as low as possible. They are NOT your advocate.

Why this matters: Adjusters may miss damage, underestimate costs, or intentionally low-ball estimates. They have no obligation to maximize your payout.

Your protection: Do not assume the adjuster's estimate is accurate. Get a second professional opinion. Document everything yourself.

2. Initial Insurance Estimates Are Almost Always Low (Usually 30-50% Underfunded)

Insurance companies use several tactics to reduce initial estimates:

  • Underestimated labor costs: They use below-market labor rates (sometimes 10-20 year old rates)
  • Incomplete damage identification: Secondary damage (water intrusion, insulation damage, structural rot) is missed
  • Aggressive depreciation: Older roofs receive 30-70% deductions for depreciation (often illegally)
  • Code upgrade avoidance: They ignore modern building codes that require upgrades
  • Removal/disposal avoidance: They don't budget for old material removal
  • Overhead & profit reduction: They underestimate contractor markup costs

Real-world example: An insurance company estimates roof replacement at $8,000. A qualified roofing contractor provides an estimate of $12,500. The difference? Missing labor hours, code upgrades, and disposal costs. Through supplements, we get the homeowner to the $12,500 amount their policy should cover.

3. You Have the Right to Your Own Estimate—Always Exercise It

Insurance policies explicitly grant you the right to obtain an independent estimate (called an "appraisal" or "independent assessment"). Most homeowners never use this right.

Why you should: An independent, quality contractor estimate provides leverage in supplement negotiations. It shows the insurance company that their estimate is inaccurate.

How it works: We provide you with a detailed, professional estimate using Xactimate software (the industry standard for insurance estimates). Your insurer must address discrepancies between your estimate and theirs.

4. Code Upgrades Are Often Covered (Even When You Don't Know It)

Modern building codes (2023 Connecticut Building Code, based on International Residential Code) have updated requirements that older roofs don't meet:

  • Wind resistance: New codes require wind-rated fastening, roof-to-wall connections, and hurricane-resistant sheathing
  • Ventilation: Attic ventilation standards have evolved; older roofs may need upgraded ventilation
  • Underlayment: Modern codes require synthetic underlayment and better moisture barriers
  • Impact resistance: Hail-resistant shingles and underlayment may be required for high-hail-risk areas

Critical fact: If your roof is damaged and must be replaced, it MUST be built to current code. Insurance companies are obligated to pay for code-required upgrades. Many insurers try to dodge this—we fight to ensure you get full coverage.

5. Replacement Cost vs. Actual Cash Value: Which Do You Have?

This is one of the most important details in your policy.

  • Replacement Cost Value (RCV): Insurance pays for full replacement at current prices, with NO depreciation deduction. This is what you want.
  • Actual Cash Value (ACV): Insurance pays replacement cost MINUS depreciation. If your roof is 15 years old and had 25-30 year life expectancy, you might get only 40-50% of replacement cost. This is devastating.

Action item: Check your policy RIGHT NOW. If you have ACV coverage, consider upgrading to RCV immediately (before any damage occurs). Most homeowners don't realize they're underinsured until it's too late.

Types of Storm Damage We Handle

Wind Damage

High winds lift and separate shingles, expose underlayment, and damage roof edges and flashing. Wind damage often goes unnoticed until water intrusion occurs weeks later.

  • Shingle lifting and separation
  • Flashing damage and water infiltration
  • Soffit and fascia damage
  • Gutter damage and separation
  • Structural damage (roof deck, trusses)

Hail Damage

Hail creates dimples and cracks in shingles and causes granule loss. Many hail damage claims are missed because homeowners don't realize small dents can cause long-term water intrusion.

  • Shingle dimpling and cracking
  • Granule loss (indicates underlying damage)
  • Metal flashing dents and punctures
  • Gutter and downspout damage
  • Skylights and vents damage

Ice Dams

Ice dams form when heat escapes through the roof, melts snow at the roof edge, and refreezes at the gutter. Water backs up under shingles and into the home.

  • Water intrusion into attic and walls
  • Interior ceiling and wall damage
  • Insulation damage and saturation
  • Structural damage from prolonged moisture

Note: Ice dam damage requires addressing both the damage AND the heat loss that caused it. Insurance may cover the damage but not the preventative fix. Code upgrades (additional ventilation, insulation) may be required.

Fallen Trees & Branches

Trees damage roofs through weight, impact damage, and constant debris.

  • Structural damage from impact
  • Shingle and underlayment damage
  • Branch punctures and abrasion
  • Gutter and flashing damage

Important: Check your homeowners policy—many policies DON'T cover tree damage if the tree was already dead or diseased. Living tree damage is typically covered.

Water Intrusion (Secondary Damage)

This is often the most significant and most missed damage type. Water intrusion causes:

  • Attic insulation damage and saturation
  • Drywall and plaster damage
  • Mold and mildew (usually not covered, but underlying water damage is)
  • Flooring and subfloor damage
  • Electrical damage and fire risk
  • Structural rot in joists and framing

Insurance companies frequently miss water damage in initial estimates because it's not visible from the outside. This is where detailed supplements are critical.

Insurance Claims FAQ: Your Questions Answered

Common Questions About the Insurance Claims Process

Q: What is a deductible and how does it affect my claim?

Your deductible is the amount YOU pay before insurance coverage applies. If you have a $1,000 deductible and damage totals $15,000, you pay $1,000 and insurance pays $14,000. Typical deductibles range from $500-$2,500. Storm-related claims sometimes qualify for lower deductibles or even waived deductibles—check your policy.

Q: Can I negotiate with my insurance company after the adjuster's estimate?

Yes, absolutely. This is why supplements exist. Insurance adjusters often provide initial estimates that are 30-50% low. You have the RIGHT to dispute the estimate and request supplements. We document additional damage, code requirements, and pricing corrections, then submit a formal supplement claim. Insurance companies are obligated to address every line item in your supplement—if they disagree, they must provide detailed justification.

Q: What if my claim is denied?

A denial means insurance believes the damage is not covered (age-related wear vs. storm damage, deliberate damage, or specific policy exclusions). Read the denial letter carefully—it must state the reason. You can:

  • Request an independent assessment to prove the damage was storm-related
  • File a complaint with the Connecticut Insurance Department
  • Appeal the denial with additional documentation and expert testimony
  • Contact a public adjuster or attorney if the claim is large enough

Q: How long does the insurance process take?

Typical timeline: 30-60 days from initial damage to approved work. This assumes one or two supplement rounds. Complex claims can take 60-90+ days. The breakdown: Days 1-3 documentation, Days 3-7 claim filing, Days 7-14 adjuster inspection, Days 14-30 estimate review, Days 30-60 supplement review and approval.

Q: What does my homeowners insurance actually cover?

Homeowners insurance typically covers damage from covered perils, which include wind, hail, falling trees, and ice dams (water damage from ice dams IS covered). NOT covered: normal wear and tear, age-related deterioration, lack of maintenance, deliberate damage, and sometimes flooding (requires separate flood insurance). Code upgrades required to meet current building code are typically COVERED when replacing damaged sections.

Q: Do I have to use the contractor my insurance company recommends?

No. You have the absolute right to choose your own contractor. Insurance companies cannot force you to use preferred contractors. Your choice of contractor is protected by Connecticut law. If an insurance company denies coverage for a contractor you choose, they must have a legitimate, documented reason (not just "they're not on our preferred list").

Q: What's the difference between "RCV" and "ACV" and which should I have?

RCV (Replacement Cost Value) pays for full replacement at current prices with NO depreciation deduction. ACV (Actual Cash Value) pays replacement cost MINUS depreciation. If your roof is 15 years old, ACV might pay 40-50% of replacement cost due to depreciation. RCV is dramatically better. If you have ACV, contact your agent immediately to upgrade to RCV (you can do this anytime, not just after damage).
